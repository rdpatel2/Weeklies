# M0: Does Weeklies save time and help people stay within budget?

Weeklies should make planning meals less work and help people avoid spending
more than they intended. A calendar full of suggestions is useful only if
checking those suggestions, fixing them, and adding up prices leaves people
better off.

For this first check, we simulated ten people doing both tasks. They browse at
different speeds, sometimes change their minds, and sometimes run out of time.
These are fictional sessions. The simulation exercises our evaluation; it does
not establish that real people benefit from Weeklies.

## What we decided to measure

The task is to plan seven dinners. The baseline is choosing meals manually from
the same menus and recording them in a blank calendar. The Weeklies condition
adds generated suggestions to review. Each person keeps the same preferences
and budget across both conditions.

Our claim is that Weeklies makes this task at least **30% faster**, measured by
the median of each person's time reduction. It must also produce complete plans
within budget for at least **80% of participants**, improving on the manual
baseline by at least **20 percentage points**. At least 80% must finish seven
suitable meals in each condition, and we need at least five paired sessions.
All checks must pass.

Those are project targets, not findings from research. Five pairs are enough to
exercise an initial check, not to estimate benefits reliably for everyone. The
evaluator uses policy `m0-time-budget-v2`; the targets remain unchanged.

## How we simulated the sessions

[scripts/simulate_m0.py](../scripts/simulate_m0.py) uses Python's standard-library
random generator with seed **510**. A seed fixes the sequence of random draws:
rerunning the same configuration gives the same fictional people and events.
Changing the seed creates a different sample. That gives us variation without
making the tutor chase a different result on every run.

Here is the procedure, in the order the script follows it.

1. **Load a shared menu.** [catalog.csv](../evals/m0/catalog.csv) contains 16
   fictional meals with prices, cuisines, vegetarian flags, and allergens.
   Prices range from $7.95 to $16.95 per serving. All restaurants are assumed
   open and all items in stock. Totals exclude taxes and fees. Repeated meals
   are allowed, so seven servings of the cheapest suitable meal establish
   whether a budget is feasible.
2. **Balance task order.** Five people do manual planning first and five do
   Weeklies first. The script shuffles those assignments. Human actions take
   8% less time in the second task to represent practice; model waits do not.
3. **Give each person consistent traits.** Browsing pace and review carefulness
   come from lognormal distributions with log-mean 0 and log-standard deviation
   0.25, clamped to 0.55–1.9 and 0.6–1.7 respectively. Budget attention is uniform
   between 0.35 and 0.9, and patience is an integer between 700 and 1,200 seconds.
   The same traits apply to both tasks. Budgets are sampled from $65, $80, $95,
   and $110. A person has a 30% chance of being vegetarian and avoids either no
   allergen, milk, peanuts, or wheat. They also have a preferred cuisine and a
   shared taste score between 0.5 and 1.5 for each meal.
4. **Choose among suitable meals.** Vegetarian and allergen requirements filter
   the menu. The script rejects a catalog if it cannot support that person's
   seven-meal budget. Within the eligible menu, the selection weight is
   `taste × cuisine factor / price in dollars ^ pressure`. The cuisine factor is
   2 for the preferred cuisine and 1 otherwise. Pressure is budget attention
   multiplied by 0.8 manually and by 2 for Weeklies. This assumes suggestions
   favour cheaper meals more strongly. It is an explicit assumption, not an
   execution of the application's budget filter or its model. An over-budget
   plan can still result.
5. **Run the manual task.** Setup takes a uniform 20–50 seconds. Each meal needs
   browsing, review, and recording. Browsing is lognormal with a median of
   60 seconds and log-standard deviation 0.5. Review is triangular with minimum
   8, maximum 35, and most likely value 18 seconds. Recording takes a uniform
   8–18 seconds. There is a 15% chance of backtracking to another choice, adding
   a uniform 30–80 seconds. Browsing, setup, and recording scale with pace;
   reviews scale with carefulness.
6. **Run the Weeklies task.** Setup takes a uniform 35–75 seconds. Generation
   waits are lognormal with a median of 45 seconds and log-standard deviation
   0.6. In 12% of sessions that wait is multiplied by a uniform 3–6 to represent
   an occasional delay. Each meal needs a triangular 10–50 second review,
   most likely 20 seconds, scaled by carefulness. Each person has a rejection
   probability sampled between 12% and 38%. A rejected suggestion adds a
   replacement search, triangular between 25 and 130 seconds with a most likely
   value of 55, scaled by pace, followed by another review. The replacement is
   accepted in this simplified model. Recording takes a uniform 5–12 seconds,
   scaled by pace.
7. **Check and sometimes repair the budget.** After seven meals, adding up prices
   takes a triangular 10–50 seconds, most likely 20, scaled by carefulness. If
   the plan is over budget, budget attention determines the probability of
   trying repairs. The person attempts one to three swaps, replacing the most
   expensive meal with a cheaper eligible choice. Each attempt adds browsing
   with lognormal median 50 seconds and log-standard deviation 0.5, scaled by
   pace, plus 15 seconds of review scaled by carefulness. Repairs stop once the
   budget is met or attempts run out. There is no guarantee of success.
8. **Apply the person's time limit.** Every action consumes time. If patience
   runs out during an action, that action is truncated and the session stops.
   A meal counts only after recording finishes; a budget swap counts only after
   the swap finishes. Already recorded meals remain in the plan. We do not
   assign an incomplete session to a particular participant in advance.
9. **Save the evidence.** The CSV records elapsed time, accepted meal count,
   budget, and the sum of selected item prices for both tasks. Each row includes
   the seed and participant reference. [simulation.json](../evals/m0/simulation.json)
   saves each person's traits, the catalog, phase durations, final item choices,
   and swap details, along with the assumptions and catalog/input SHA-256 hashes.
   Time is accumulated in hundredths of a second so the event totals reconcile
   with the CSV.

The shape of these distributions lets a few searches or waits take much longer
than usual. Their values are still guesses. We have not measured actual browsing
speeds, generation latency, rejection rates, or practice effects. Sharing traits
makes the pairs more coherent, but it cannot make invented observations real.

## How the evaluator scores the sample

[scripts/eval_m0.py](../scripts/eval_m0.py) validates the CSV before calculating
anything. It rejects missing fields, duplicate participants, invalid times or
costs, and mixed synthetic/pilot files. Times must be positive and no more than
1,200 seconds; meal counts must be 0–7. Budgets and costs use integer cents.

An incomplete plan receives a time score of **1,200 seconds**, even if its person
stopped earlier. That person stays in the sample. In seed 510, `sim-09` stops
manual planning after six meals at 849 seconds, so its manual score is 1,200.

For each pair, the evaluator calculates:

```text
time reduction = 1 - (Weeklies scored seconds / manual scored seconds)
```

For example, `sim-01` takes 992.88 seconds manually and 888.77 with Weeklies,
a reduction of 10.49%. The evaluator takes the median of all ten paired
reductions, rather than dividing the two groups' median times.

A budget success requires seven suitable meals and a total at or below the cap.
A cheap partial plan fails. Rates use every participant, and the improvement is
measured in percentage points. The evaluator consumes the recorded meal counts
and totals; it does not independently inspect suitability or prices.

## What happened

The default sample is saved in [synthetic.csv](../evals/m0/synthetic.csv), with
its full evaluation in [result.json](../evals/m0/result.json).

| Check | Seed 510 result | Target | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Median time reduction | 24.67% | At least 30% | Fail |
| Weeklies complete plans within budget | 9/10, or 90% | At least 80% | Pass |
| Improvement over manual budget success | 20 points: 90% versus 70% | At least 20 points | Pass |
| Suitable-plan completion | Manual 90%; Weeklies 100% | At least 80% each | Pass |
| Paired sessions | 10 | At least 5 | Pass |

The overall result is **FAIL** because the time target is missed. This result is
about the assumed behaviour, not actual Weeklies performance.

We also ran the five consecutive seeds **510–514**, keeping all five rather
than choosing the best outcome. [sensitivity.json](../evals/m0/sensitivity.json)
contains their reports under the same policy.

| Seed | Median time reduction | Manual within budget | Weeklies within budget | Overall |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 510 | 24.67% | 70% | 90% | FAIL |
| 511 | 12.78% | 30% | 60% | FAIL |
| 512 | 42.54% | 50% | 80% | FAIL |
| 513 | 40.28% | 60% | 80% | PASS |
| 514 | 38.11% | 30% | 60% | FAIL |

Only one sample passes every check. Seed 512 misses the manual completion target
even though time and budget checks pass. These runs show sensitivity to sampled
people and events; they are not confidence intervals or a test of whether our
assumptions describe real users. Changing the assumptions could change all five.

## How to reproduce the numbers

Run from the repository root with **Python 3.12**, matching the evaluation job.
The instrument uses only the standard library and needs no database or API key.

```bash
python -m unittest discover -s scripts/tests -v
python scripts/simulate_m0.py --seed 510 \
  --trace /tmp/m0-simulation.json \
  --sensitivity-output /tmp/m0-sensitivity.json > /tmp/m0-synthetic.csv
python scripts/eval_m0.py /tmp/m0-synthetic.csv --output /tmp/m0-result.json
```

The last command exits with **1**, the valid negative hypothesis result. Exit 0
means every check passes; exit 2 means an input or file error. After that command,
compare each saved file:

```bash
diff -u evals/m0/synthetic.csv /tmp/m0-synthetic.csv
diff -u evals/m0/simulation.json /tmp/m0-simulation.json
diff -u evals/m0/sensitivity.json /tmp/m0-sensitivity.json
diff -u evals/m0/result.json /tmp/m0-result.json
```

All comparisons should be empty. The 17 tests cover evaluator boundaries,
incomplete sessions, malformed input, exit codes, seeded reproduction, trace
and price reconciliation, dietary eligibility, balanced order, deadline
truncation, and an impossible catalog. To explore another sample, supply a
different `--seed`; `--participants` controls its size. The sensitivity output
always keeps five consecutive seeds starting with the supplied seed.

## What we would do next

Our decision for this synthetic review is **re-plan the next experiment**. Keep
the time-saving and budgeting goals and their thresholds. The useful next step
is to replace assumed timings and choices with observations from at least five
intended users. Randomness makes the demonstration less rigid; observation is
what will make it informative about the product.

Each person should perform both tasks with the same menu snapshot, preferences,
and budget. Set the budget before either task and check that a suitable
seven-meal plan is feasible. Alternate task order and use comparable date ranges.
Start timing when the task is presented, include generation waits and corrections,
and stop at completion or 20 minutes. Retain abandoned and incomplete sessions.

An observer should check the seven date slots against preferences, allergens,
availability, and restaurant hours. Record itemized prices, taxes, and mandatory
fees under the same fulfillment method, without placing paid orders. Save those
observations with anonymous session IDs and evidence references. Label those CSV
rows `pilot`, keep them separate from synthetic data, and use the same evaluator
without moving the thresholds.
